We are the sandwich generation. You have probably heard that term. Caught between caring for aging parents and still being needed by children who may or may not be fully launched. But here is the part the term does not capture: for Black women, the sandwich is not just generational. It is cultural, financial, emotional, and physical all at once. And we are carrying it largely alone and mostly in silence.

## The numbers tell the *story*

Black Americans are significantly more likely than white Americans to provide unpaid care for an aging family member. Black women in particular are disproportionately represented among family caregivers — often stepping in because professional care is too expensive, because no one else will, or because our culture has taught us that this is simply what you do.

According to AARP, family caregivers spend an average of 24 hours per week providing care. For those in intensive caregiving situations, that number climbs above 40 hours — a second full-time job, unpaid and largely invisible. The financial impact is staggering. Women who take on caregiving roles lose an average of $324,000 in wages, Social Security benefits, and retirement savings over their lifetimes.

## The grief nobody *names*

Caring for an aging parent is a particular kind of grief called anticipatory grief. You are grieving the parent they used to be while they are still here. You are watching someone who was once your protector, your authority, your safe place — become someone who needs you to protect them. The role reversal is disorienting in a way that is genuinely difficult to prepare for.

> That resentment that sometimes flickers up — the one you immediately feel guilty about — is not a character flaw. It is a signal. It is your nervous system telling you that you are running beyond capacity.

## What sustainable caregiving actually *looks like*

The airplane oxygen mask principle applies here. You cannot sustain this if you have nothing left. Taking care of yourself is not selfish caregiving. It is the only kind that lasts.

That means accepting help. Even when it is imperfect. Even when the other person does not do it exactly the way you would. Imperfect help extended is more valuable than perfect help withheld.

It means using the resources that exist. Medicaid covers a significant range of in-home care services for those who qualify. Many communities have Area Agencies on Aging that provide free or reduced cost services. The ARCH National Respite Network can help you find respite care in your area.

And it means getting your own support. Caregiver support groups exist specifically for this — spaces where you can say what you cannot say anywhere else.

## The love underneath the *exhaustion*

I do not want this post to lose sight of what is real here. Underneath the exhaustion, underneath the grief that is hard to name, there is love. Profound, complicated, deep love for the people who raised you.

That love is why you are doing this. And that love is worth protecting, which is exactly why you cannot let it burn you down to nothing.

Taking care of yourself inside this caregiving season is an act of love — for your parent, for your family, and for the woman you still need to be when this chapter is over.
