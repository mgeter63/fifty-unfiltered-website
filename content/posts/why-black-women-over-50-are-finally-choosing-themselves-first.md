Let me tell you something I wish somebody had told me twenty years ago.

You cannot pour from an empty cup. I know you have heard that before. But here is the part nobody finishes the sentence with: if you spend your whole life filling everybody else's cup, one day you will reach for yours and find out it has been bone dry for so long that you forgot it was even yours to fill.

I see it in women every single day. We hit fifty and we look up from everything we have been doing — raising children, holding marriages together, building careers, taking care of aging parents, being the strong one in every room — and we realize we have been so busy showing up for everyone else that we never learned how to show up for ourselves.

According to a 2023 study published in the journal Women's Health, Black women are significantly more likely than other groups to report feeling emotionally depleted and undervalued in their personal relationships. Sixty-two percent of Black women surveyed said they consistently prioritize the needs of others over their own. Sixty-two percent, sis. That is not a personality trait. That is a pattern. And patterns can be broken.

## The moment everything shifted for *me*

I remember sitting in my car in a parking lot after a particularly exhausting family gathering. I had cooked, cleaned, mediated a disagreement between two relatives who were not even speaking to each other, consoled someone going through a breakup, and listened to everyone's problems for six hours straight. And when it was over, when everyone went home satisfied and full, I sat in that car and I cried. Not because I was sad. Because I realized I had not eaten a single plate of the food I spent four hours preparing. I had not sat down once. Nobody had asked me how I was doing. Not once.

That was the moment I understood. I had become so good at being everything for everyone that I had made myself invisible. And the worst part? I had helped them do it.

## Why we do *this*

This is not about blame. Understanding why we do something is not the same as excusing it. But we need to be honest about where this pattern comes from.

Black women have been socialized for generations to be the backbone of everything and everyone around them. We learned early that being strong was survival. We learned that our needs were secondary. We internalized messages — from our families, from society, from every space we walked into — that our value was tied to what we could produce for others. How much we could endure. How little we needed.

Dr. Cheryl Woods-Giscombé, a researcher at the University of North Carolina, developed the concept she calls the Strong Black Woman schema — the idea that Black women feel culturally pressured to be self-reliant, emotionally suppressed, and always there for everyone else. Her research found that this pattern directly contributes to higher rates of stress, anxiety, and physical health problems in Black women. We are literally making ourselves sick trying to be strong for everybody else.

Fifty is the place where a lot of us finally get tired enough to stop.

## What choosing yourself actually *looks like*

I want to be clear about something. Choosing yourself is not selfish. It is not abandoning the people you love. It is not becoming someone cold or closed off. It is learning that you are also one of the people you are responsible for loving well.

It looks like saying no without a three-paragraph explanation.

It looks like making the appointment you have been putting off for yourself, before you make everyone else's.

It looks like not answering the phone at eleven o'clock at night when you know the call is going to drain you for two days.

It looks like having the conversation you have been avoiding because you were more worried about the other person's comfort than your own truth.

It looks like buying yourself the thing. Taking the trip. Enrolling in the class. Starting the thing you said you would do when you had more time, and understanding that the time is now because now is all any of us actually have.

## The relationships that survive this *shift*

Here is what I have noticed. When you start choosing yourself, one of two things happens with the people around you. Either they adjust and the relationship gets better, more honest, more mutual. Or they resist, because they were benefiting from a version of you that was giving everything and asking for nothing.

Both outcomes are information.

The relationships worth keeping are the ones that can handle the real you. The one with needs. The one with limits. The one who sometimes says I cannot right now. Those relationships, the ones that survive your becoming, are the ones that were real all along.

> At fifty and beyond, we do not have time for relationships that only work when we are running on empty.

You have spent enough years being everyone's everything. You are allowed to be your own something. You are allowed to take up space, to have desires, to protect your energy, to rest without guilt, to want more for yourself than you have settled for.

Fifty is not the beginning of the end. For a lot of us, it is the beginning of the first chapter we ever wrote for ourselves.

Pick up the pen, sis. It is your story now.
