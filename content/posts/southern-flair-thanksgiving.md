## Pull Up a Chair

Let me say this up front. This is not a diet post. Thanksgiving comes one day a year, and in my kitchen we cook with butter.

This is a Southern flair Thanksgiving. I'm walking you through the whole table the way we do it down here: the turkey, homemade cornbread, cornbread dressing, fried cabbage, macaroni and cheese and candied yams.

I give you amounts so you have a place to start. After that, trust your tongue. Taste as you go, add a little more, and taste again. That is how good cooks have always done it.

At the end I lay out a game plan for the week, so you are not standing at the stove from sunup Thursday morning.

## Butter Rubbed Roast Turkey

Serves 10 to 12. The most important thing with a turkey is getting under the skin to the real meat. That is where this butter rub goes, the night before.

Some folks brine their turkey overnight. I have never brined a turkey, and mine still comes out juicy.

What you need:

- 1 whole turkey, 12 to 14 pounds, thawed
- 1 1/2 sticks salted butter, softened
- 1 tablespoon chicken flavored seasoning, like McCormick's. This is what makes your rub.
- 1 tablespoon all-purpose seasoning
- 2 teaspoons garlic powder
- 2 teaspoons onion powder
- 2 teaspoons black pepper
- 1 teaspoon salt
- 1/4 teaspoon cayenne pepper, if you want it. A little goes a long way.
- 1 large onion, cut in quarters
- 1 large green bell pepper, cut in big pieces

How to make it:

1. Thaw the turkey in the refrigerator, never on the counter. The USDA says to allow about 24 hours for every 4 to 5 pounds. A 14 pound bird needs about three days, so get it in the fridge by Sunday.
2. Wednesday night, pull out the neck and the bag of giblets. Pat the turkey dry inside and out with paper towels.
3. Mash the butter with the chicken seasoning, all-purpose seasoning, garlic powder, onion powder, black pepper, salt and cayenne, if you're using it. Go easy on the salt, since the butter and the all-purpose seasoning bring some of their own.
4. Slide your fingers under the skin of the breast and thighs to loosen it. Push about two thirds of the butter under the skin, down to the meat, and spread it around. Rub the rest all over the outside. Cover it and let it sit in the fridge overnight.
5. Thursday morning, take the turkey out about 30 minutes before it goes in. Heat the oven to 400°F.
6. Stuff the cavity with the onion and bell pepper. These are for flavor only. Your dressing bakes in its own pan.
7. Set the turkey breast side up on a rack in a roasting pan, so it roasts up out of the juices. Tuck the wing tips under the body.
8. Roast at 400°F for 15 to 20 minutes. Then drop the oven to 285°F and let it slow cook. Don't turn it and don't fuss with it.
9. Go by a meat thermometer, not the clock. Low and slow takes longer, and the time depends on the size of your bird. The turkey must reach 165°F in the thickest part of the thigh. Check the breast and the wing too. It looked done. But it's not.
10. Let it rest 20 minutes before you carve. Save every drop of those pan drippings for your gravy.

You won't find lemon, thyme or rosemary on my turkey. If you can find black garlic, use it in place of some of the garlic powder. It gives your turkey a different flavor, and folks say it is better for you.

## Cornbread Dressing: The Center of the Table

Dressing is the center of my family's Thanksgiving. The turkey gets the picture. The dressing is what everybody comes for.

I grew up one of five girls, and between us girls and my mom, we lived in that kitchen. The whole community wanted to eat our food. My mom was the boss in that kitchen. She has passed on now, but she will always be the head chef.

Truth be told, I wasn't the one doing the cooking. I was the one pulling everything. The meal, the flour, the eggs, the onions, the sage. So I knew what went into every dish, and I knew what didn't.

Pulling all those ingredients taught me that quality matters. When somebody tells you there's no difference between this butter and that butter, or this sugar and that sugar, don't believe it. It tastes different. It cooks up different. It heats up different. Buy the best you can.

Dressing is a mixture. Once you bring it all together, you can't separate it out again. Life is the same way. When you start doing things, you can't undo them. So put good things in.

Dressing is family. You've got Aunt Susie with the bad mouth, Uncle John with his drinking, the cousin that steals and the other cousin that lies about everything. You've got your sister who gets on your last nerve, and your brother, the overprotective one who stands in like a father. Daddy is hit or miss, so you never know if he's coming. But when you bring them all together in one pot, your mama is the egg that makes everything stick together. You must respect each other, and you must love each other. Even when you dislike them, you won't let anyone else break that dressing up.

### First, the Cornbread

Back then we used Martha White self-rising meal. I'm sticking with Martha White here because you can find it in most any grocery store. Self-rising meal already has what it needs to rise, so there's no baking soda, no baking powder and no sugar. And no bacon grease. Nobody is putting bacon grease in their cornbread these days.

What you need:

- 2 cups Martha White self-rising cornmeal mix
- 1 tablespoon self-rising flour
- 1 large egg, or 2 if you are making a big batch
- 1 cup milk
- Vegetable oil

How to make it:

1. Put a little vegetable oil on a paper towel and wipe the bottom and sides of a 10 inch cast iron skillet. That conditions the skillet so your cornbread won't stick.
2. Set the empty skillet in the oven and heat the oven to 360°F. Let the skillet heat about 10 minutes.
3. Pour about a tablespoon of vegetable oil into the hot skillet and put it back in the oven to get that oil hot.
4. Don't mix your batter too early. This is where people miss it. Self-rising meal and flour start working as soon as the milk hits them, so you cannot let that batter sit on the counter. When the oil is about 5 minutes from hot, beat the meal, flour, egg and milk together well.
5. You don't want your batter sloppy or thin. You want it a little runny, so it has time to settle into the pan.
6. Take the skillet out with a good oven mitt. Pour the hot oil into your batter and mix it in.
7. Pour it all into the skillet and put it straight into the oven.
8. Bake about 30 minutes, until you have a golden brown top.
9. Crumble it into a big bowl, cover it loosely with a clean towel and let it dry out overnight. Dry cornbread soaks up the broth better.

### Then, the Dressing

Serves 10 to 12. Bake it in its own pan, not inside the bird.

What you need:

- 1 skillet of cornbread from above, crumbled and dried
- 2 large eggs, beaten, or 3 to 4 if you are feeding a big crowd
- 1 large onion, chopped
- 1 large green bell pepper, chopped
- 1 cup chopped celery, if you like it. I don't, so I leave it out.
- 1 stick butter
- 4 to 5 cups chicken or turkey broth
- 1 can (10.5 ounces) cream of celery or cream of chicken soup, if that is how your family makes it
- A pinch of rubbed sage
- Salt and black pepper to taste

How to make it:

1. Heat the oven to 350°F. Butter a 9 by 13 baking dish.
2. Put the crumbled cornbread in your biggest bowl.
3. Melt the butter in a skillet over medium heat. Cook the onion and bell pepper, plus the celery if you use it, until soft, about 8 to 10 minutes.
4. Scrape the vegetables and every bit of that butter into the cornbread. Stir in the soup if you are using it.
5. Add the broth a cup at a time, stirring as you go, until it looks like thick, wet oatmeal. It should look wetter than you think. It firms up in the oven.
6. Add your pinch of sage, then salt and black pepper. Taste it now, before the eggs go in, and fix it if it needs it.
7. Stir in the eggs and spread it all in the dish.
8. Bake 45 to 55 minutes, until the top is golden brown and the middle is set. A food thermometer in the center should read 165°F.

You won't find thyme or rosemary in my dressing. You have sage, and sage is the mother. Sage can make your dressing or break it. A lot of people don't understand how strong it is. You don't need a tablespoon. You need a pinch, the kind you pick up between your fingers and sprinkle in, and that's it. That can of sage from the grocery store will outlast the date on the bottom, because you only use a pinch at a time.

One more thing about your peppers. Some folks buy the bag of frozen chopped bell peppers to save time. Look before you use them. I have bought bags this year that looked fine on the outside and were spoiled on the inside. Fresh peppers you chop yourself are worth the few extra minutes.

## Southern Fried Cabbage

Serves 6 to 8. Keep it simple: butter, onion and garlic. Make it last so it goes to the table hot.

What you need:

- 1 large head green cabbage, about 3 pounds
- 4 tablespoons butter
- 1 medium onion, sliced
- 1 clove garlic, minced. You only want a hint of it.

How to make it:

1. Cut your cabbage however you like it. Rinse it and let it drain.
2. Put the butter in a big cast iron skillet over medium heat and let it get hot.
3. Saute your onion and garlic first, until the onion is soft.
4. Then put your cabbage in the skillet. It will look like too much. It cooks down. Stir it now and then until it is as tender as you like it.

Cabbage means something in our community. Ask the older folks and they will tell you that green is money. That is why so many Black families put cabbage and greens on the table New Year's Day, for prosperity in the year ahead.

## Five Cheese Macaroni and Cheese

Serves 8. This one is baked, and it takes five cheeses. The amounts are adapted from Spend With Pennies' baked mac and cheese.

Now, this five cheese one is the bougie mac and cheese. If you want a country Southern mac and cheese, use red rind cheese, that hoop cheese with the red wax on the outside.

What you need:

- 1 pound elbow macaroni
- Salt for the pot
- 5 tablespoons butter, plus a little more for the baking dish
- Black pepper and a little salt
- About 3 cups half and half
- About 1 pound shredded cheese in all, about 4 cups: Gouda, Gruyere, Havarti, mozzarella and just a little cheddar

How to make it:

1. Boil your noodles in salted water. Some people like chicken broth instead, for a different flavor. No oil in that pot.
2. Don't overcook the pasta, because it still has to go in the oven. Go by the directions on the package.
3. When the noodles are done, take them off the stove, drain them and put them in a bowl. Don't put your butter in the skillet until the noodles are out of the pot.
4. Now melt the 5 tablespoons of butter in a skillet with the black pepper and a little salt. Pour in the half and half and let it get warm.
5. Melt in half of your cheese. Melt your cheese into the warm half and half on low heat so the sauce is smooth before it hits the noodles. Too much heat makes it grainy.
6. Pour the sauce over the noodles and mix.
7. Butter your baking dish, spread the macaroni in it and put the other half of the cheese on top.
8. Bake at 365°F for about 11 minutes. Everything is already done. All you are doing is bringing it together.

Make it ahead so you aren't standing in the kitchen all day. I put mine together Tuesday or Wednesday and keep it in the refrigerator. Don't freeze it if you're making it a day or two early. Freezing can leave the sauce less silky. On Thanksgiving, bring it out, let it come to room temperature, then bake it 20 to 30 minutes at 365°F.

## Candied Yams

Serves 4 to 6. Bake these with the dressing at 350°F.

What you need:

- 2 of the biggest sweet potatoes you can find
- 4 tablespoons butter, half a stick
- About 2/3 cup packed light brown sugar
- 1 teaspoon vanilla paste

How to make it:

1. Heat the oven to 350°F. Butter an 8 by 8 baking dish.
2. Peel the sweet potatoes and slice them into rounds about 1/2 inch thick. Lay them in the dish, overlapping a little.
3. Melt the butter in a saucepan over medium heat. Stir in the brown sugar until it melts, about 2 to 3 minutes. Take it off the heat and stir in the vanilla paste.
4. Pour the syrup over the potatoes. Cover the dish tight with foil and bake 45 minutes.
5. Take off the foil and spoon the syrup over the top. Bake another 25 to 30 minutes, spooning syrup over them every 10 minutes, until fork tender and the syrup is thick.
6. Let them sit 10 minutes before serving. The syrup keeps thickening as it cools.

## My Game Plan for the Week

Spread the work out and Thursday feels like a holiday instead of a shift.

- Sunday: Put the frozen turkey in the refrigerator to thaw.
- Tuesday: Bake the cornbread. Crumble it and let it dry overnight. Make one last run to the store.
- Wednesday: Rub the turkey with the butter rub. Chop your onion and bell pepper for the dressing. Put together the macaroni and cheese and keep it in the refrigerator.
- Thursday morning: Turkey goes in the oven at 400°F, then down to 285°F to slow cook.
- When the turkey comes out: Turn the oven to 350°F. Mix the dressing and bake it next to the candied yams. Keep the turkey under foil while it rests and you carve it.
- Last 30 minutes: Fry the cabbage. Bake the macaroni and cheese at 365°F.

A few more things I have learned at the stove:

- I use salted butter in every recipe here. If yours is unsalted, add a pinch more salt.
- Add salt at the end, after you taste. Broth brings salt of its own.
- Cast iron does the best job on cornbread and cabbage. If you don't have it, use your heaviest pan.
- Get a food thermometer. It takes the guessing out of the turkey and the dressing.
- Put leftovers in the refrigerator within 2 hours of cooking. The USDA says they keep 3 to 4 days in the fridge, or 3 to 4 months in the freezer.

Cook with love, taste as you go, and fix a plate for somebody who can't make it to your table this year. Happy Thanksgiving from my kitchen to yours.
