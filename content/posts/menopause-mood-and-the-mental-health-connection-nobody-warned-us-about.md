Let me ask you something. In the last two years, have you felt more anxious than usual? Had moments of deep sadness that seemed to come out of nowhere? Found yourself snapping at people you love over small things and then wondering who that woman was? Lying awake at three in the morning with your mind racing even though your body was exhausted?

If you said yes to any of those, your doctor may have tested your thyroid. Checked your vitamin D. Suggested you try to stress less. But here is what they may not have connected it to: menopause.

The mental health symptoms of menopause are some of the most underdiagnosed and undertreated experiences in women's medicine, particularly for Black women. And the consequences of that gap are real.

## What is actually happening in *your body*

Estrogen does not just regulate your menstrual cycle. It plays a significant role in the regulation of serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine — the neurotransmitters most closely linked to mood, motivation, and emotional stability. When estrogen levels decline during perimenopause and menopause, those neurotransmitter systems are directly affected.

This is why the mood changes of menopause are not "just in your head" in the dismissive sense of that phrase. They are literally in your brain chemistry. Your brain is responding to a dramatic hormonal shift, and for some women, that shift is destabilizing in ways that look exactly like clinical depression or an anxiety disorder.

> According to the North American Menopause Society, women are two to four times more likely to experience a major depressive episode during the menopause transition than at any other time in their adult lives.

Two to four times. And yet most of us were never told this was coming.

## Why Black women are hit harder and *helped less*

Research consistently shows that Black women report more severe menopausal symptoms than white women, including more intense mood symptoms and hot flashes that disrupt sleep more significantly. Chronic sleep disruption alone is a major driver of mood disorders.

At the same time, Black women are less likely to receive hormone therapy or mental health treatment for menopausal symptoms. We are less likely to be believed when we describe our symptoms. The combination of more severe symptoms and less treatment is a gap that has real consequences for our quality of life.

## The symptoms worth paying *attention to*

Beyond the hot flashes and irregular periods, here is what menopause can actually look like from a mental and emotional standpoint: anxiety that feels new or worse than before, irritability that seems out of proportion to what triggered it, sadness or a flatness that is hard to explain, difficulty concentrating or remembering things, a loss of motivation or pleasure in things you used to enjoy, sleep disruption that compounds every other symptom, and feeling unlike yourself in a way that is hard to articulate.

## What you can *do*

Talk to your doctor and be specific. Do not just say you have been feeling off. Say: I am experiencing mood changes, anxiety, and sleep disruption, and I want to discuss whether this could be related to perimenopause or menopause. Bring a list of symptoms.

Hormone therapy is not right for everyone, but it is more nuanced than the fears that circulated in the early 2000s suggested. Have a real conversation with your provider about the risks and benefits for your specific situation.

Lifestyle changes matter. Exercise, particularly strength training and cardio, has been shown to significantly reduce menopausal mood symptoms. Sleep hygiene matters enormously.

And please, find a doctor who takes you seriously. You deserve a provider who listens to the full picture of what you are experiencing.

Menopause is a transition, not a permanent state. You deserve to understand what is happening to you, and you deserve care that reflects that.
